The One Thing That Stops Most Hacks Dead

Why two-factor authentication is the single biggest security upgrade you can make today — and it's free.

Every year, millions of business accounts get hacked — not because of sophisticated attacks, but because someone guessed or stole a password. Here's the uncomfortable truth: passwords alone aren't enough anymore.

Two-factor authentication (2FA) means that even if someone gets your password, they still can't get in without a second code that only you have — usually from an app on your phone.

How it works

You enter your password as normal. Then you're asked for a 6-digit code that changes every 30 seconds on your phone. No code = no access.

Turn it on for your email first — that's the master key to everything else. Then your bank, accounting software, and anywhere else you store important business data.

  • Gmail: Settings → Security → 2-Step Verification
  • Microsoft 365: Account → Security Info → Add sign-in method
  • Free apps: Google Authenticator, Authy

Why Your Business Needs a Backup — and How to Know If Yours Actually Works

A backup you haven't tested is not a backup. Here's what real protection looks like.

Ransomware attacks on small businesses doubled last year. When they hit, you have two choices: pay the ransom or restore from backup. Only one of those options doesn't involve giving money to criminals.

The 3-2-1 rule: Keep 3 copies of important data, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 copy offsite (like the cloud).

Warning sign

If you can't name exactly where your backup is and when it last ran, you probably don't have one that works.

Most small businesses have no backup at all, or a backup they've never tested. Testing means actually restoring a file — not just assuming the backup ran because a light turned green.

  • Run a test restore of a real file every month
  • Make sure backups run automatically — daily at minimum
  • Store at least one copy somewhere other than your office

How to Spot a Phishing Email Before It's Too Late

Phishing is the #1 way hackers get into small businesses. Learn the warning signs in under 5 minutes.

Phishing emails are fake messages designed to trick you into clicking a bad link or giving up your password. They're getting better every year — some are nearly impossible to tell from the real thing.

Here's what to look for before you click anything:

  • Check the sender's actual email address — "support@paypa1.com" is not PayPal.
  • Hover over links before clicking — the URL shown at the bottom of your screen should match where you'd expect to go.
  • Urgency is a red flag — "Your account will be closed in 24 hours!" is a pressure tactic to make you act without thinking.
  • Unexpected attachments — if you didn't ask for a file and don't recognize the sender, don't open it.
Golden rule

When in doubt, don't click the link. Go directly to the website by typing the address yourself.

The Password Problem: Why "Strong" Passwords Aren't Enough Anymore

And what actually works to keep your accounts safe without going crazy trying to remember everything.

Most people use the same password (or a slight variation of it) across multiple accounts. This means one data breach at any website can unlock your email, your bank, and your business software all at once.

The solution isn't memorizing 50 different passwords. It's using a password manager — a secure app that creates and remembers strong, unique passwords for every site.

Free options

Bitwarden is free and open source. 1Password and Dashlane are excellent paid options (~$3/month). All are far better than a spreadsheet.

  • Use a different password for every account — your password manager does this automatically
  • Make your master password a long phrase, not a word: "MyDog$Ran4Miles" is stronger than "P@ssw0rd!"
  • Never write passwords in a document called "passwords.txt"
