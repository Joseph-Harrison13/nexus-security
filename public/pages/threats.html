Threat #1 — Phishing Email

The Fake Bank Security Alert

This is one of the most common scams. The email looks just like a real message from your bank — same logo, same style. It only takes one click to fall for it.

From: security-alerts@firstnational-secure.com
To: you@yourbusiness.com
⚠️ Urgent: Suspicious login detected on your account
Today at 9:14 AM
🏦 FirstNational Bank

Dear Valued Customer,

We have detected a suspicious login attempt on your business account from an unrecognized device located in Lagos, Nigeria.

To protect your account, we have temporarily limited access. You must verify your identity within 24 hours or your account will be permanently suspended.

Verify My Account Now →

This message was sent to you@yourbusiness.com. If you did not request this, please click here to report.

© 2026 FirstNational Bank, N.A. | 123 Financial Blvd, Chicago, IL 60601 | Unsubscribe

1
The sender's address is fake. It says firstnational-secure.com — not your bank's real web address. Scammers set up look-alike addresses just for the scam.
2
It tries to scare you into rushing. "24 hours or your account is closed" is meant to make you panic and act without thinking. A real bank gives you time — and calls you.
3
The button is a trap. Clicking "Verify" takes you to a fake copy of your bank's login page. Whatever you type — your username, password, even your card number — goes straight to the scammer.
4
It doesn't use your name. Your real bank knows your name and uses it. "Dear Valued Customer" means they have no idea who you are.
What it can cost you

A restaurant owner clicked "Verify" and typed in her login. Within 20 minutes, scammers moved $6,400 out of her account. Her bank got $1,200 back — the other $5,200 was gone for good. That's payroll, rent, or a month of profit, gone in one click.

Threat #2 — Business Email Compromise

The Fake Vendor Invoice

A scammer pretends to be a company you already pay — a supplier, a contractor. They wait until you're expecting a bill, then send a fake one that asks you to pay a new bank account (theirs).

From: billing@officesuppIies-co.com
To: accounts@yourbusiness.com
Invoice #4471 — Payment Due
Today at 2:03 PM

Hi,

Please find attached Invoice #4471 for $2,850.00 for the office supplies order delivered on June 15th.

Please note our banking details have changed as of this month. Kindly update your records and use the new account below for this payment:

Bank: First Commerce Bank
Account Name: Office Supplies Co LLC
Routing: 021000021
Account: 7743920184

Please confirm receipt of this email so we can mark the invoice as pending payment.

Best regards,
Jennifer Walsh
Accounts Receivable — Office Supplies Co

1
One letter is off. Look closely: it's a capital I in "suppIies," not a lowercase l. On a screen they look the same — that's the whole trick.
2
"Our bank details have changed" is the #1 line scammers use. Before you ever pay a new account, call the company on a number you already have — never the number in the email.
3
It wants you to reply. "Please confirm you got this" is bait — if you reply, the scammer knows a real person is reading and handling the money.
What it can cost you

A dental office paid $2,850 to the "new" account. Two weeks later the real supplier called asking why they hadn't been paid. By then the money had been moved through several accounts overseas — impossible to get back.

Threat #3 — IT Impersonation

The Fake IT Department Email

A scammer looks up who does your tech support (often right on LinkedIn), then emails your staff pretending to be them. People click because the name looks familiar.

From: mike.henderson@yourbusiness-helpdesk.com
To: all-staff@yourbusiness.com
ACTION REQUIRED: Mandatory Microsoft 365 Re-authentication
Today at 8:47 AM

Hi everyone,

As part of our scheduled security upgrade, all staff must re-authenticate their Microsoft 365 accounts by end of business today or email access will be suspended until manual verification can be completed.

This takes less than 2 minutes. Please click the link below and log in with your current work credentials:

https://microsoft365-reauth.yourbusiness-helpdesk.com/login

If you have any questions, reply to this email.

Thanks,
Mike Henderson
IT Support — Your Business

Sent from IT Helpdesk Portal v3.2

1
The email is from outside your company. Your real IT sends from @yourbusiness.com. This one is @yourbusiness-helpdesk.com — close, but not yours.
2
Everyone must act "today." Real IT changes are planned ahead, not rushed. The deadline is only there to stop you from pausing to double-check.
3
The link steals your password. It has your company's name in it to look real, but it goes to the scammer. Type your password there and they get into your email — and everything linked to it.
4
Questions "reply here" go to the scammer. If you email back to check, you're talking to the crook — not your real IT person.
What it can cost you

At a small law firm, seven people typed in their passwords before anyone thought to call IT. The scammer got into the email, quietly redirected a client's $31,000 payment to themselves, and copied two years of private client files. Money lost, trust broken, and a possible lawsuit.

Threat #4 — SMS Phishing (Smishing)

The Fake Delivery Text

Scam texts are everywhere now, because people let their guard down on their phones. These look like a delivery update from UPS, FedEx, or the post office.

Messages — USPS_NOTIFY
USPS: Your package could not be delivered due to an incomplete address. A $1.35 redelivery fee is required. Claim within 24 hours: usps-delivery-resc.com/track
Failure to update will result in the package being returned to sender.
1
The link isn't the real company. The post office only uses usps.com. Anything else is fake.
2
The tiny "fee" is the hook. $1.35 feels harmless — so you type in your card. But that page grabs your full card number, expiry, security code, and address.
3
It rushes you again. "Claim within 24 hours" is pressure to act fast. Real delivery notices don't expire like that.
What it can cost you

A shop owner was actually expecting a package, so the $1.35 "fee" seemed normal. She used her business debit card. By the next morning, $1,240 in fake charges had gone through from three different countries.

Threat #5 — CEO / Boss Fraud

The Urgent Wire Transfer Request

A scammer pretends to be the boss and messages whoever handles the money. They pile on pressure and secrecy — "I need this now, and keep it between us."

From: David Chen <david.chen.ceo@gmail.com>
To: sarah@yourbusiness.com
Quick favor — confidential
Today at 4:58 PM

Sarah,

I'm currently in a client meeting and can't talk. I need you to handle an urgent wire transfer for me. This is time-sensitive — the deal closes tonight.

Amount: $8,500
Please wire to:
TD Bank · Routing: 026013673 · Acct: 4492817364

I'll explain everything when I'm out of the meeting. Please don't call me — I'll be unavailable for the next 2 hours. Just reply here to confirm you've sent it.

David
Sent from my iPhone

1
It's not the boss's real email. Your boss uses their work address. A Gmail or Yahoo address asking for money is a big red flag.
2
"Don't call me" is the biggest warning sign. A real boss can always be reached. Telling you NOT to call is how the scammer stops you from checking.
3
"Keep it secret" + "do it now" is a trap. Together they stop anyone else from noticing and saying "wait, that's not right."
4
The reason is vague on purpose. "The deal closes tonight" sounds believable but gives you nothing to check — a 30-second call would expose it.
What it can cost you

A bookkeeper at an HVAC company wired $8,500 after getting an email just like this. She texted the owner two hours later to confirm — he was home on the couch, never sent it. They called the FBI. The money was never recovered.
